What was the challenge?

A US-based life-sciences manufacturer had begun attracting European customers without a dedicated regional sales, distribution, or manufacturing infrastructure. Unsolicited European orders were about 1% of revenue.

The opportunity was promising, but expansion introduced difficult commercial questions. Which products were generating demand? How competitive was the company against established European suppliers? Were international shipping costs undermining its pricing advantage? And at what point would local distribution or manufacturing become financially viable?

Our six-person consulting team developed a phased European market-entry strategy. My contributions focused on analyzing existing market performance, benchmarking competitors, evaluating pricing economics, and developing the distributor-partnership and local-manufacturing phases of the expansion plan.

How did the competitive landscape shape our positioning?

I benchmarked 14 competitors across five product categories, comparing product overlap, catalog breadth, customization capabilities, pricing position, and European market presence. The analysis revealed a strategic gap between large, premium-priced suppliers with extensive distribution networks and smaller, price-driven competitors with more limited capabilities.

The most compelling opportunity was not competing with global incumbents on scale or with commodity suppliers on price. It was targeting customers who valued flexible customization, smaller batches, and faster turnaround.

That positioning helped inform which customer segments, products, and distribution partnerships deserved priority. I also profiled 103 bio and life-science companies across seven countries, grouped into three target profiles. The best-fit accounts cluster in Germany, the UK and Switzerland, so early conference and distributor effort goes there.

Why wasn’t competitive pricing enough?

My commercial pricing analysis compared US product prices with European distributor pricing and local competitors.

The findings showed that a competitive manufacturing price could become substantially less attractive once international freight, distributor markups, and fulfillment costs were added. One product was marked up about 270% in EU distribution.

This shifted the strategic question from “How should we price our products?” to “How should we redesign the distribution model to make our prices competitive?”

How could we enter Europe without overinvesting?

I developed the distributor-partnership phase of the strategy, evaluating regional distributor networks, commercial channels, product suitability, and shipping economics.

Rather than recommending an immediate European subsidiary, I proposed a more capital-efficient approach: establish local inventory through a distribution partner once demand and reorder patterns justified the investment.

The strategy prioritized high-turnover catalog products and European biotech clusters, with the option to expand through a multi-country distributor group or focus more narrowly on the DACH region.

When would local manufacturing make sense?

I also developed the longer-term local manufacturing phase, exploring contract manufacturing partnerships and strategic acquisitions.

The objective was to preserve the manufacturer’s customization and fast-turnaround advantage while reducing the limitations of shipping made-to-order products from the United States.

I evaluated potential partners against product portfolio overlap, quality certifications, manufacturing capabilities, customer fit, and acquisition feasibility.

The defining principle was that each phase should generate the evidence required to justify the next investment. Distribution would address near-term shipping economics, while manufacturing would become relevant when recurring demand and custom-product requirements warranted greater commitment. Each phase has success metrics that produce the evidence needed to fund the next, structured around the Three Horizons framework.

What did I deliver?

I contributed the commercial analysis and expansion recommendations underpinning the proposed European go-to-market strategy:

  • An assessment of existing European customers, retention, and product performance.
  • Competitive benchmarking across 14 suppliers and five product categories.
  • Pricing and logistics analysis identifying opportunities to improve delivered-cost competitiveness.
  • A distributor-partnership strategy with channel options, geographic priorities, and proposed performance measures.
  • A longer-term manufacturing and acquisition framework for scaling European operations.

The final deliverable was a strategic recommendation, not an executed market launch. Its value was providing the client with a structured, evidence-based path for evaluating European expansion while managing capital requirements and operational risk.

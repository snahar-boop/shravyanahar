What was the challenge?

Ahead of the 2026 FIFA World Cup, we wanted to investigate whether historical team performance and squad characteristics could reliably predict a national team’s pre-tournament FIFA points.

Could we estimate team strength without using FIFA rank itself as a predictor?

Working as a two-person analytical team, Isabela Ract Pousada and I explored whether recent wins, goals, squad market value, and historical World Cup performance could explain differences in national team standings.

Our objective was not simply to generate rankings, but to build and evaluate a predictive model that could generalize beyond its training data.

What did I own?

I collaborated with Isabela on the end-to-end regression modeling process, from predictor selection and multicollinearity diagnostics to model comparison, validation, and interpretation.

Our work included:

  • Evaluating 20 initial variables and refining the dataset to 10 predictors.
  • Identifying and removing redundant variables through multicollinearity diagnostics.
  • Building and comparing linear regression, K-nearest neighbors (KNN), regression trees, bagging, and boosting models.
  • Evaluating predictive performance using R², RMSE, and training-versus-validation results.
  • Selecting a final model and applying it to estimate pre-tournament FIFA points for 48 national teams.
How did we approach the problem?

We used a Kaggle dataset containing historical performance and squad-level information for the 48 national teams in the 2026 World Cup dataset.

We deliberately excluded FIFA rank because it was directly related to the points we were trying to predict. Including it would have undermined the purpose of identifying independent performance signals.

We then conducted multicollinearity diagnostics to identify overlapping predictors. For example, recent wins and goals scored contained redundant information, as did recent losses and goals conceded.

After refining our variable set, we retained 10 predictors covering recent performance, squad characteristics, and historical tournament experience.

To evaluate our models consistently, we partitioned the data into 60% training and 40% validation sets.

Which model performed best?

We compared five supervised learning approaches to understand their predictive accuracy and ability to generalize.

Linear regression achieved a training R² of 0.860 but dropped to 0.420 on validation data, indicating substantial overfitting.

Base KNN achieved the highest validation R² of 0.811, but its perfect training fit raised concerns about memorization.

We ultimately selected KNN Boosting, which achieved a validation R² of 0.806 and RMSE of 71.65. Although its validation accuracy was marginally lower than base KNN, we favored its less-perfect training fit and ensemble approach.

The key decision was balancing predictive accuracy with concerns about overfitting rather than automatically selecting the model with the highest R².

What did the data reveal?

Our analysis identified two particularly important predictors of pre-tournament FIFA points.

Recent wins: Wins over the previous four years were statistically significant (p = 0.003), highlighting the relationship between recent competitive performance and team standing.

Squad market value: Total squad market value was also statistically significant (p = 0.009) and appeared as the first decision split in our regression tree.

By comparison, average squad age was not statistically significant (p = 0.794).

These findings suggested that recent performance and squad investment were stronger indicators of pre-tournament standing than some other characteristics in our dataset.

What were the results?

We applied our selected model to the 48-team dataset to generate predicted pre-tournament FIFA points.

Our model ranked France first with 1,868.82 predicted points, narrowly ahead of Spain at 1,867.92 points.

England, Argentina, and Germany completed our predicted top five.

The results demonstrated how historical performance and squad-level characteristics could be used to estimate FIFA points without directly incorporating FIFA rank as an input.

Importantly, these were predictions of pre-tournament FIFA points, not match results or the eventual World Cup champion.

What would I do differently?

Our biggest limitation was the small dataset of 48 national teams, which restricted how confidently we could assess generalization.

If I revisited the project, I would expand the dataset across multiple tournament cycles and use repeated cross-validation to evaluate model stability.

I would also investigate how sensitive our results were to different training and validation splits before selecting a final model.

What did I learn?

The model with the highest validation accuracy isn’t necessarily the model you should select.

This project strengthened my understanding of feature selection, multicollinearity, overfitting, and predictive model evaluation.

More importantly, it reinforced the importance of understanding a model’s limitations before using its outputs to support decisions.

For me, the biggest takeaway was that effective analytics requires more than producing accurate predictions. It requires knowing when those predictions can be trusted.

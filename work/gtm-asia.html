How do you build a profitable consumer brand across four very different Asian markets?

Over seven simulated years, our team developed and executed a go-to-market strategy for Allsmile, a consumer oral-care brand expanding across Asia. We competed against two other teams while balancing market share, profitability, brand equity, and long-term growth.

I independently built an AI-assisted analytics dashboard that became our team’s central decision-making tool, helping us translate complex performance data into actionable strategies each round.

What was the question?

Expanding internationally isn’t simply about entering the largest markets or selling the most products. Each market presents different consumer preferences, competitive dynamics, distribution structures, advertising requirements, and manufacturing economics.

Our challenge was to determine where to compete, which products to introduce, how to allocate resources, and when to change course, all while maintaining profitability.

We managed the brand across China, India, South Korea, and the Philippines, making interconnected decisions about product portfolios, pricing, advertising, distribution, salesforce allocation, and manufacturing investment.

With new performance reports arriving after each simulated year, the challenge was not a lack of data. It was turning fragmented data into clear, timely decisions.

What did I own?

I independently designed and built an interactive, AI-assisted business intelligence dashboard, consolidating 18 simulation data sheets into a centralized decision-support tool.

Rather than reviewing individual reports in isolation, I created a way for our team to evaluate performance across markets, compare strategic alternatives, and identify priorities for the next round.

The dashboard brought together:

  • Market performance: Revenue, market share, profitability, and brand equity across countries and years.
  • Product analytics: SKU-level sales, gross margins, product performance, and portfolio opportunities.
  • Competitive intelligence: Competitor market shares, positioning, and relative performance.
  • Resource allocation: Advertising, distribution, salesforce, and manufacturing economics.
  • Strategic recommendations: Market-specific opportunities, risks, and proposed next steps based on the latest results.

I updated the dashboard as the simulation progressed, and my teammates actively used it to evaluate trade-offs and guide our decisions.

How did I decide where to invest?

I evaluated each market through three lenses: profitability, growth potential, and brand strength. Using my dashboard, I compared SKU margins, market share, competitive positioning, and investment requirements to identify where our team should invest, optimize, or pull back.

This helped us avoid treating revenue growth as the only measure of success. Instead, we evaluated the trade-offs between short-term performance and long-term profitability.

How did the data influence our strategy?

China: Investing in long-term profitability. China offered significant revenue potential, but manufacturing and logistics costs created pressure on margins. Our team invested in local manufacturing, fundamentally changing the economics of serving the market.

The dashboard helped us track the impact of this decision through manufacturing costs, SKU profitability, and contribution margins. China’s gross margin increased from approximately 6% to 25% over the simulation.

South Korea: Rethinking an underperforming market. South Korea initially struggled with profitability. Rather than continuing the same approach, our team restructured advertising investment, expanded distribution, and shifted toward higher-margin Whitening products.

The market moved from a loss-making position to sustained profitability, demonstrating the importance of adapting strategy based on performance rather than continuing to invest in an ineffective approach.

Philippines: Prioritizing margin over volume. In the Philippines, SKU-level analysis revealed that a Healthy product had a gross margin of just 3.3%.

Our team discontinued the product and focused on the Whitening portfolio. By Year 6, gross margin had increased from 14.3% to 18.0%, even as unit sales declined.

This was an important strategic trade-off: maximizing sales volume wasn’t necessarily the best way to maximize business value.

How did it turn out?

By the end of the seven-year simulation, our team achieved:

  • $924.3M in cumulative net regional contribution, the highest among the three competing teams.
  • $3.59B in Year 7 manufacturer sales.
  • 17.8% final market share.
  • 77 brand equity score.
  • 141.5% cumulative return on marketing investment.
  • 91.1 final weighted score, ranking first among the three teams.

These were collective team outcomes within a business simulation, supported by the strategic decisions we made throughout the seven years.

What did I learn?

The biggest lesson was that good strategy depends on more than having access to data. It requires identifying which metrics matter, understanding how decisions interact, and making trade-offs with incomplete information.

Building the dashboard reinforced something central to product management: analytics is most valuable when it changes what a team decides to do next.

It also showed me the value of using AI tools to accelerate analytical workflows while retaining human judgment over recommendations, assumptions, and business decisions.
